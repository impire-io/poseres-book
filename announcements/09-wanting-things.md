## LinkedIn

Every result in the book up to this chapter was produced by a brain that wanders at random. Every action it took was a coin flip. Random has one virtue nothing else has: it can't fool you, because it has no preferences to bias what the brain gets to see.

Then I gave it something to want. The obvious choice, and the one with the research pedigree, is curiosity: prefer what you haven't seen, weighted by where your predictions have been improving. At the reference scale it tied with random. At scale it was worse, in seven of eight seeds, while its policy did exactly what it was designed to do. Eighty-seven percent of its actions were directed, all that direction was real, and the sum of it was negative.

Five controlled experiments later, the problem was not directedness. A content-free directed policy, the same statistical structure of directedness coupled to nothing about novelty, scored neutral. The harm was the content. In a world where everything is learnable, novelty-seeking spreads a finite life as thinly as possible across the state space: maximum coverage, minimum depth. The inverted preference, seek the familiar, beat random cleanly, and the shipped drive prefers what it is already getting good at.

Also in the chapter: why the drive is the one component in this system that is forbidden to learn.
