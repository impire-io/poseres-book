## LinkedIn

Chapter 3 ended with the engine reporting that every large world had one dimension. The natural assumption is that something is broken. Weeks of measurement said otherwise. Every component was doing exactly what it was designed to do, in a world it had been tuned for: true size 3, ten sensor channels. Six constants were quietly about that world without anyone having said so.

One of them is the protected childhood a new frame gets before eviction can touch it. Stretch it stepwise, 2 to 12 to 24 to 29 cycles, and the discovered dimensionality climbs stepwise with it: means of 4.7, 5.7, 6.7, 10.7. Repair all six, then a fair judge, a corrected survival bar and a frame-aging fix on top of them, and the system lands at a median of 10 dimensions on a world of true size 20. Nine on 35. Nine on 50. Half the truth or less, and the reason turned out to be the most useful thing the project has produced.

With the distortions gone I could plot honest error against dimensionality directly. There is no elbow at 20. Error just falls, smoothly, each added dimension buying a smaller improvement than the one before. This world's observations are a nonlinear image of its 20-dimensional latent, and under that map the image simply isn't a 20-dimensional object. The truth leaves no signature for any analysis to find.

What the parsimony term does instead is charge rent. Each dimension costs a flat fee, selection keeps buying while the improvement beats the fee, and the measured crossing point sits at dimensions 8 to 12. The system lands at 10 and holds there at every scale and budget I've measured. The map is not sized to the territory. It's sized to the mapmaker's budget.
